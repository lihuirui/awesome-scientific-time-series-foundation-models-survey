\section{Scientific Reasoning LLMs and Autonomous Agents}
\label{sec:reasoning_llms}

\subsection{The Paradigm Shift: From Numerical Fitting to Scientific Reasoning}
While foundation backbones excel at numerical continuous pattern matching, complex scientific discovery necessitates logical reasoning, physical hypothesis formulation, and contextual interpretation. Early attempts to adapt general LLMs directly to time series via numerical text prompting (e.g., feeding raw floating-point numbers as text tokens) incurred severe limitations: token sequence inflation, numerical precision truncation, and inability to capture continuous derivatives~\cite{jin2024whatcan}. Consequently, modern research has bifurcated into two primary paradigms: (1) unified continuous tokenization for scientific time series, and (2) autonomous multi-agent tool orchestration.

\subsection{Unified Continuous Tokenization: SciTS and TimeOmni}
Wu et al.~\cite{wu2025scits} introduced \textbf{SciTS}, the first large-scale cross-disciplinary benchmark designed specifically to evaluate LLMs on scientific time series understanding and generation. Spanning 12 natural science disciplines (including meteorology, astrophysics, neuroscience, and physical chemistry) across 43 specialized tasks and over 50,000 instances, SciTS exposed the failure modes of standard prompting. To resolve these deficiencies, the authors proposed \textbf{TimeOmni}, a unified architecture featuring a multi-scale continuous patch tokenizer that projects 1D and multi-dimensional scientific series into dense semantic embeddings aligned with pre-trained LLM latent spaces. This enables foundation LLMs to engage in multi-step reasoning, such as identifying solar flare triggers from light curves, attributing anomalous climate oscillations, and generating conditioned time series forecasts with physical explanations.

\subsection{Frequency-Aware Contextual Integration: ClimateLLM}
Rather than treating atmospheric series as unstructured text, Li et al.~\cite{li2025climatellm} introduced \textbf{ClimateLLM}. This framework decomposes multi-station and gridded meteorological sequences into orthogonal frequency bands via Fourier transform modules. The spectral components are encoded as prefix prompt embeddings that condition a frozen 7B LLM backbone, effectively leveraging the LLM's long-context attention mechanism to model multi-scale temporal teleconnections and localized extreme events.

\subsection{Domain-Specialized Foundation LLMs: K2 for Geoscience}
Adapting general-purpose LLMs to specialized Earth sciences necessitates injection of structured geological and environmental knowledge. Deng et al.~\cite{deng2024k2} introduced \textbf{K2}, a foundational language model tailored for geoscience knowledge understanding and utilization. Pretrained on over 5.5 billion tokens of peer-reviewed geoscience literature, stratigraphic catalogues, and temporal sensor records, K2 bridges the terminology gap between general text corpora and specialized geophysical nomenclature. Through the \textit{GeoSignal} instruction-tuning protocol, K2 learns to reason across temporal geological processes, infer lithological fault dynamics, and assist researchers in interpreting complex spatio-temporal sensor logs with physical groundedness.

\subsection{Multimodal Vision-Language Grounding: GeoChat}
Satellite and aerial Earth observation series frequently require aligning visual changes with natural language queries. Kuckreja et al.~\cite{kuckreja2024geochat} developed \textbf{GeoChat}, the first grounded large vision-language model explicitly engineered for remote sensing. Built upon a Vicuna-1.5 language backbone coupled with a high-resolution CLIP ViT-L/14 visual encoder via LoRA adaptation, GeoChat processes multi-spectral satellite imagery and temporal revisit pairs. Crucially, GeoChat extends standard captioning by generating spatial bounding coordinates directly in text token sequences, enabling fine-grained temporal change detection (e.g., urban expansion, flood inundation progression, and forest fire burn scar expansion) with conversational natural language explanations.

\subsection{Reprogramming Pretrained Language Models: Time-LLM}
Rather than training domain transformers from scratch or fine-tuning entire parameter spaces, Jin et al.~\cite{jin2024timellm} introduced \textbf{Time-LLM}, pioneering the paradigm of reprogramming frozen large language models for time series forecasting. Time-LLM recognizes that pre-trained language backbones (such as LLaMA-7B or GPT-2) possess exceptional cross-modal pattern recognition and sequence abstraction capabilities. To align modalities, Time-LLM:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Input Patch Reprogramming}: Divides continuous input series into patches and projects them into the word token embedding space through a linear reprogramming layer conditioned on cross-attention with textual domain prototypes.
    \item \textbf{Prompt-as-Prefix Conditioning}: Augments the reprogrammed patches with domain task instructions (e.g., dataset semantics, sampling frequency, and trend descriptions) as natural language prefix prompts.
    \item \textbf{Frozen Backbone Inference}: Keeps the foundational LLM weights entirely frozen during training, tuning only lightweight linear adapters.
\end{enumerate}
By reprogramming frozen language models, Time-LLM achieves superior few-shot and zero-shot performance across scientific and benchmark datasets, proving that language model representations serve as universal priors for temporal dynamics.

\subsection{Autonomous Multi-Agent Climate Workflows: ClimateAgent}
Moving beyond single-model inference, Li et al.~\cite{li2025climateagent} introduced \textbf{ClimateAgent}, an autonomous multi-agent orchestration framework for complex climate data science. Scientific investigations routinely require ingesting multi-terabyte NetCDF/Zarr climate datasets, executing specialized meteorological software (e.g., Climate Data Operators (CDO), MetPy, xarray), calculating thermodynamic diagnostics (e.g., convective available potential energy, geostrophic balance), and synthesizing diagnostic reports. ClimateAgent organizes frontier LLMs into a collaborative hierarchy:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Plan-Agent}: Decomposes high-level natural language scientific research goals into rigorous directed acyclic task dependency graphs (DAGs).
    \item \textbf{Data-Agent}: Navigates distributed scientific data archives (Copernicus Climate Data Store, NASA Earthdata) to retrieve reanalysis and satellite products, managing chunking and caching.
    \item \textbf{Coding-Agent}: Synthesizes and executes Python/Bash diagnostic code in secure sandboxed environments with automated error recovery loops, iteratively inspecting stack traces to debug array shape and temporal coordinate alignment errors.
    \item \textbf{Critic-Agent}: Verifies physical plausibility, evaluating dimensional unit consistency and testing for energy conservation bounds before assembling peer-review-ready figures and reports.
\end{itemize}
Evaluated on the standardized \textit{Climate-Agent-Bench-85}, ClimateAgent demonstrated an 82\% task completion rate without human intervention, marking a concrete transition from static numerical forecasters to self-directed AI co-scientists capable of hypothesis formulation and autonomous verification in natural Earth systems.

\subsection{Tool Invocation Protocols and Execution Sandboxes for Scientific Co-Scientists}
\label{sec:agent_tool_interfaces}
As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:agent_reasoning_dag}, the operational viability of autonomous scientific co-scientists hinges upon rigorous DAG scheduling, stateful computational sandboxes, dynamic error recovery loops, and physical plausibility guardrails:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Hierarchical DAG Task Scheduling}: When presented with a complex scientific inquiry (e.g., assessing the dynamic intensification of an atmospheric river or Mediterranean marine heatwave), the Plan-Agent formulates a topological Directed Acyclic Graph. Nodes represent modular atomic subtasks (data query, regridding, thermodynamic flux computation, kinetic energy budget integration), while directed edges enforce data dependencies.
    \item \textbf{Structured Tool Invocation \& Distributed Ingestion}: Scientific foundation agents communicate with computational tools via structured JSON schema function calls. Rather than generating unconstrained code, the Data-Agent triggers domain-specific API endpoints (e.g., Copernicus Marine Toolbox, ECMWF CDS API, USGS Hydro-Data) and manages out-of-core sharding through \texttt{xarray} and \texttt{dask}, preventing memory exhaustion when handling multi-terabyte spatio-temporal datasets.
    \item \textbf{Stateful Computational Sandboxes}: Executing scientific workflows requires isolated REPL (Read-Eval-Print Loop) environments equipped with verified domain libraries:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Atmospheric \& Earth Observation}: \texttt{xarray} and \texttt{dask} for out-of-core chunked tensor operations; \texttt{MetPy} for thermodynamic diagrams and potential vorticity inversions; \texttt{rasterio} and \texttt{rioxarray} for satellite raster projections.
        \item \textit{Ocean Dynamics}: \texttt{oceanbench} and \texttt{copernicusmarine} client APIs for automated GLORYS and altimetry verification.
        \item \textit{Geophysics}: \texttt{ObsPy} and \texttt{SeisBench}~\cite{woollam2022seisbench} for streaming waveform filtering, phase picking, and seismic moment tensor inversions.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Traceback Self-Correction Loop}: Scientific data formats frequently exhibit idiosyncratic edge cases: inverted latitude coordinate ordering ($90^\circ \to -90^\circ$ vs. $-90^\circ \to 90^\circ$), non-standard calendars (e.g., 360-day climate model years in CMIP6), or incompatible vertical pressure unit conventions ($\text{Pa}$ vs. $\text{hPa}$). As depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:agent_reasoning_dag} (Loop 4), when the Python runtime emits an exception or dimension mismatch, the agent automatically captures the execution traceback, inspects array metadata (via \texttt{ds.dims} and \texttt{ds.coords}), and performs self-reflection to iteratively synthesize corrected code blocks.
    \item \textbf{Physical Plausibility Verification Guardrails}: Even syntactically error-free code may produce unphysical scientific outputs. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:agent_reasoning_dag} (Stage 5), the Critic-Agent acts as a domain-specific gatekeeper: evaluating strict conservation of mass and energy, checking non-negativity of physical quantities (precipitation $P \ge 0$, solar radiation $R \ge 0$), verifying bounded relative humidity ($0\% \le \text{RH} \le 100\%$), and enforcing dimensional consistency across derived units before approving publication-grade artifacts.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Closed-Loop Autonomous Scientific Discovery and Empirical Coding Benchmarks}
\label{sec:closed_loop_discovery}
Moving beyond passive question answering and isolated diagnostic script synthesis, the frontier of scientific reasoning aims for fully autonomous, closed-loop scientific experimentation and discovery:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Autonomous Multi-Agent Scientific Discovery: The AI Scientist}: Lu et al.~\cite{lu2024aiscientist} introduced \textbf{The AI Scientist}, the first comprehensive system for fully automated open-ended scientific discovery driven by frontier foundation LLMs. Rather than executing predefined static scripts, The AI Scientist autonomously completes the entire scientific research lifecycle:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Novelty Search \& Ideation}: Generates novel research hypotheses and cross-references academic literature via the Semantic Scholar API, verifying that proposed scientific angles have not been previously investigated.
        \item \textit{Autonomous Experimentation \& Code Modification}: Clones experimental repositories, dynamically modifies neural architectures or numerical solvers, executes experiments in isolated sandboxes, and collects empirical logs.
        \item \textit{Manuscript Generation \& Visualization}: Formulates a complete, publication-grade LaTeX paper complete with experimental plots, mathematical definitions, and properly cited bibliography entries.
        \item \textit{Automated Peer Review}: Deploys an automated LLM reviewer agent calibrated against standard conference review criteria (e.g., ICLR/NeurIPS rubrics), scoring experimental soundness, significance, and empirical rigor to provide iterative refinement feedback.
    \end{itemize}
    This paradigm illustrates how scientific reasoning agents can transition from mere computational assistants to autonomous research partners capable of steering complex simulation experiments.
    \item \textbf{Multi-Step Scientific Numerical Reasoning Benchmark: SciCode}: While general-purpose coding benchmarks (e.g., HumanEval, MBPP) evaluate standard algorithmic manipulation, they fail to probe the nuanced numerical rigor demanded by scientific disciplines. To establish an authoritative benchmark, Tian et al.~\cite{tian2024scicode} released \textbf{SciCode}, an expert-curated benchmark comprising 338 multi-step scientific coding problems developed by natural scientists spanning Physics, Fluid Dynamics, Materials Science, and Applied Mathematics. Each problem decomposes complex physical phenomena (e.g., Navier--Stokes vorticity advection, quantum wavepacket dispersion, crystal lattice dislocations) into sequential mathematical sub-equations requiring numerical integration (Runge--Kutta, symplectic integrators), boundary condition handling, and multidimensional tensor manipulation in \texttt{scipy} and \texttt{numpy}. Benchmark results reveal that while frontier models achieve over 85\% on standard software engineering benchmarks, their pass rates drop to 20--35\% on SciCode, exposing critical weaknesses in continuous derivative propagation, coordinate frame consistency, and numerical stability in stiff dynamical systems.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Closed-Loop Disaster Strategy Optimization: Hierarchical Agents with Knowledge Graphs}
\label{sec:hierarchical_disaster_agents}
In physical disaster response and hazard mitigation, foundation LLMs face three acute operational barriers: multi-objective trade-offs under dynamic spatio-temporal constraints, non-stationary environmental shifts, and semantic hallucination leading to execution failure. Addressing these vulnerabilities in urban hydrological emergencies, Ji et al.~\cite{ji2025entropy} proposed \textbf{H-J}, a hierarchical multi-agent framework integrating domain Knowledge Graphs (KG) with entropy-constrained strategy optimization.

H-J decouples real-time perception, global strategic planning, and operational actuation across three collaborative agent tiers:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Perception \& Knowledge Grounding}: Continuous sensor streams (water level gauges, radar precipitation fields, road network edge velocities) are mapped into an RDF-based urban knowledge graph, enforcing hard physical boundaries on water routing and node capacities.
    \item \textbf{Entropy-Constrained Strategy Generation}: To prevent semantic instability, candidate response plans generated by the LLM are evaluated against an information-theoretic entropy regularizer:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{H}(\pi) = -\sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \pi(a \mid s) \log \pi(a \mid s),
    \end{equation}
    which bounds policy dispersion and prevents erratic shifts between successive decision cycles during acute flood surges.
    \item \textbf{Closed-Loop Simulation Feedback}: Candidate traffic diversion and evacuation schedules are evaluated in an offline hydrodynamic simulator before physical dispatch, with execution logs returned to the agent for recursive plan refinement.
\end{enumerate}
When tested under extreme 100-year storm surges in complex metropolitan topologies, H-J achieved a $28.4\%$ reduction in urban network congestion duration and a $34.2\%$ higher task success rate compared to state-of-the-art multi-agent reinforcement learning (PPO) and static emergency rulebooks, demonstrating how domain knowledge graphs and entropy bounds enable reliable, grounded decision-making by LLM agents in critical natural science infrastructure.

\subsection{Spatio-Temporal Causal Discovery Agents for Climate Teleconnections}
\label{sec:causal_discovery_agents}

Global climate teleconnections---such as the El Ni\~no--Southern Oscillation (ENSO), the North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO), and Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD)---couple regional weather extremes across planetary scales via Rossby wave trains and atmospheric circulation cells. However, standard data-driven foundation models and correlational analyses struggle with non-stationarity, secular climate change trends, and unobserved confounders, frequently mistaking spurious statistical correlations for true physical causation.

\subsubsection{Theoretical Foundations of Causal Discovery on Variable Groups}
In Earth system science, continuous physical fields are inherently distributed across spatial grid cells, vertical atmospheric levels, and temporal lags. Treating every spatial grid cell as an independent univariate random variable creates an intractable combinatorial search space, destroying the statistical power of causal discovery algorithms. To resolve this, Wahl, Ninad, and Runge~\cite{wahl2023groupcausal} established the rigorous theoretical foundations of \textbf{Causal Discovery on Groups of Variables}.

Let $\mathcal{V} = \{X^1, \dots, X^d\}$ denote a micro-level system of variables partitioned into disjoint macro-groups $\mathcal{V}_G = \{G_1, \dots, G_M\}$ (e.g., spatial clusters representing regional sea surface temperature anomalies or atmospheric pressure centers). Wahl et al. formulated the group-level directed acyclic graph (DAG) $\mathcal{G}_G = (\mathcal{V}_G, \mathcal{E}_G)$ and analyzed the conditions under which the Causal Markov Condition and Causal Faithfulness hold under group aggregation. In time series causal discovery, group conditional independence is tested across temporal lags $\tau \ge 1$:
\begin{equation}
    G_{i, t-\tau} \perp\!\!\!\perp G_{j, t} \mid \mathcal{S}_{t},
    \label{eq:group_cit}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{S}_t \subset \mathcal{V}_{G, t}^- \setminus \{G_{i, t-\tau}\}$ is a conditioning set of past group states. Implemented via \textbf{Group-PCMCI} in the Tigramite framework, group-level causal discovery eliminates micro-level edge redundancies, controls false discovery rates under severe auto-correlation, and reconstructs robust macro-scale climate teleconnection DAGs directly from gridded reanalysis tensors.

\subsubsection{Large Language Models as Conditional Independence Oracles (LLM-CIT)}
While constraint-based causal algorithms (such as PC and PCMCI) provide asymptotic convergence guarantees, their empirical reliability on finite observational records is severely bottlenecked by statistical Conditional Independence Tests (CIT). When conditioning sets $\mathcal{S}$ contain high-dimensional climate variables or when observational records are short, statistical tests incur high type-II error rates (failing to reject false independence) and catastrophic sensitivity to measurement noise.

Addressing this fundamental data-efficiency bottleneck, Cohrs et al.~\cite{cohrs2024llmcausal} introduced \textbf{LLM-CIT}, exploring foundation Large Language Models as domain-expert oracles for constraint-based causal discovery. Rather than relying solely on noisy empirical data to evaluate independence queries, the framework queries a foundation LLM through structured scientific prompts:
\begin{quote}
    \small \texttt{"Given conditioning set $\mathcal{S} = \{\text{Equatorial Pacific SST}, \text{Aleutian Low SLP}\}$, is $X = \text{Subtropical Jet Velocity}$ conditionally independent of $Y = \text{California Atmospheric River Precipitation}$?"}
\end{quote}
To mitigate stochastic sampling variability and semantic hallucination, Cohrs et al. introduced a \textbf{statistical voting schema} that aggregates responses across temperature-sampled query ensembles with explicit thresholding:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{I}(X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid \mathcal{S}) = \mathbb{I}\left( \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^M \text{Oracle}_m(X, Y \mid \mathcal{S}) \ge \gamma_{\text{vote}} \right),
    \label{eq:llm_cit_voting}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_{\text{vote}} \in [0, 1]$ tunes the trade-off between false-positive (spurious edge retention) and false-negative (true causal edge pruning) error rates.

Crucially, by inspecting Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning traces, the authors demonstrated that frontier LLMs do not simply retrieve memorized correlations, but actively deduce atmospheric teleconnection pathways: identifying Rossby wave train dispersion, upper-tropospheric divergence, and moisture advection dynamics to justify their conditional independence decisions. When integrated into the constraint-based PC algorithm, knowledge-based LLM-CIT provides a powerful structural prior that dramatically prunes spurious edges in data-sparse scientific regimes, demonstrating that foundation reasoning LLMs can serve as complementary domain-expert oracles for scientific causal inference.

\subsection{Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning for Adaptive Observational Targeting and Online State Corrections}
\label{sec:multi_agent_data_assimilation}

Traditional data assimilation (DA) frameworks (e.g., 3D/4D-Var, Ensemble Kalman Filters) operate under rigid, decoupled assumptions: observations are gathered passively along pre-planned satellite tracks or fixed station networks, while numerical PDE integration and statistical filtering execute in batch mode. However, catastrophic meteorological events---such as rapid hurricane intensification, atmospheric river landfalls, and localized convective microbursts---demand \textbf{adaptive observational targeting} and \textbf{closed-loop interactive assimilation}. In this regime, autonomous observing platforms (e.g., targeted dropsonde releases from crewed reconnaissance flights, uncrewed aerial systems, autonomous ocean gliders) must dynamically navigate to sampling regions of maximum expected variance reduction, while data assimilation solvers must apply real-time, physics-consistent state corrections directly to the evolving dynamical core.

\subsubsection{Ensemble Members as Autonomous RL Agents: RL-DAUNCE}
In highly non-linear, chaotic fluid dynamics, ensemble data assimilation faces severe challenges: traditional Kalman-based update steps assume Gaussian prior error distributions, leading to filter divergence, catastrophic variance collapse, and non-physical state excursions when tracking multi-modal or intermittent turbulent phenomena. 

To resolve these non-Gaussian breakdown modes, Behnoudfar and Chen~\cite{behnoudfar2025rldaunce} established \textbf{RL-DAUNCE} (Reinforcement Learning-Driven Data Assimilation with Uncertainty-Aware Constrained Ensembles). The fundamental conceptual breakthrough of RL-DAUNCE is reformulating ensemble filtering as a multi-agent reinforcement learning problem: rather than relying on a linearized empirical covariance update, each individual ensemble member $m \in \{1, \dots, M\}$ is governed by an autonomous RL agent with an individualized policy network $\pi_{\theta_m}(\mathbf{a}_t \mid \mathbf{s}_t)$. The state $\mathbf{s}_t = [\mathbf{x}_{t, m}, \bar{\mathbf{x}}_t, \mathbf{y}_t]$ combines the current ensemble trajectory, the ensemble mean, and sparse observations $\mathbf{y}_t$, while actions $\mathbf{a}_t$ represent localized state increments.

Crucially, to prevent unphysical state drift, RL-DAUNCE implements an uncertainty-aware primal-dual optimization framework enforcing hard physical constraints (e.g., tracer non-negativity, total mass conservation, and physical boundary conditions):
\begin{equation}
    \max_{\theta} \min_{\boldsymbol{\lambda} \ge \mathbf{0}} \; \mathbb{E}\left[ \sum_{t=0}^T \gamma^t r(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t) \right] - \sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k \left( \mathbb{E}[c_k(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t)] - d_k \right),
    \label{eq:rldaunce_primal_dual}
\end{equation}
where $r(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t) = -\|\mathbf{y}_t - \mathcal{H}(\mathbf{x}_{t, m} + \mathbf{a}_t)\|_{\mathbf{R}^{-1}}^2$ rewards observation tracking, $c_k(\cdot) \le d_k$ specifies physical conservation boundaries, and $\lambda_k$ are dynamic Lagrange multipliers penalizing constraint violations. By advancing multiple interacting policy agents that balance reward maximization with rigorous constraint satisfaction, RL-DAUNCE reduces state estimation RMSE by \textbf{34.2\%} over standard EnKF under strongly non-Gaussian turbulent regimes, maintaining exact boundary constraint compliance without filter divergence.

\subsubsection{Online Model-Agent Coupling in Flagship NWP: Nath et al.}
While idealized RL frameworks demonstrate mathematical convergence, scaling autonomous reinforcement learning agents to full operational numerical weather prediction models has long remained an elusive grand challenge due to the immense computational scale and strict numerical stability requirements of operational dynamical cores.

Addressing this milestone, Nath et al.~\cite{nath2026online} achieved the first successful implementation of \textbf{Online Reinforcement Learning in the UK Met Office Unified Model (UM)} through distributed model-agent coupling. Operating inside the operational global NWP atmosphere, the authors architected a high-throughput, low-latency communication layer coupling the Fortran dynamical core to PyTorch Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG) agents across distributed MPI ranks via in-memory Redis key-value stores.

The DDPG agent acts on rank-local atmospheric columns across all 70 vertical model levels, learning a continuous state correction policy $\pi_\phi(\mathbf{a}_t \mid \mathbf{s}_t)$ that outputs bounded potential-temperature tendency increments $\Delta \theta_v(z)$ to counteract systematic model parameterization drift. Trained online via nudging calculations against the Met Office operational analysis, the coupled agent maintained \textbf{unconditional numerical stability} over extended prognostic integrations. In $+6$-hour global forecast evaluations, the coupled RL agent reduced geopotential height $Z_{500}$ Mean Absolute Error (MAE) across four of six global latitude bands (achieving an impressive \textbf{14.6\%} error reduction in tropical latitudes) while simultaneously decreasing Mean Sea Level Pressure (MSLP) errors across three bands. This landmark study demonstrates that distributed autonomous RL agents can execute stable, real-time closed-loop state corrections within operational weather prediction cores, bridging the gap between autonomous agent reasoning and physical Earth system simulators.
